## 1. Neurobiology: Koob's three-stage cycle, dopamine, CRF/dynorphin anti-reward, GABA/NMDA neuroadaptation, kindling

### Takeaway
Koob and Volkow's model describes addiction as a cycle that intensifies over time: **binge/intoxication** (basal ganglia, dopamine and opioid peptides), **withdrawal/negative affect** (extended amygdala, loss of dopamine reward function plus recruitment of brain stress systems such as CRF and dynorphin/KOR), and **preoccupation/anticipation** (prefrontal cortex and insula, glutamatergic dysregulation, craving). In AUD specifically, long-term drinking leads to compensatory GABA-A downregulation and NMDA upregulation. These changes underlie tolerance and the hyperexcitable withdrawal state.

### Cited Findings
- Koob & Volkow (Neuropsychopharmacology 2010, "Neurocircuitry of Addiction"): "collapsing the cycles of impulsivity and compulsivity yields a composite addiction cycle composed of three stages — binge/intoxication, withdrawal/negative affect, preoccupation/anticipation". The stages interact, intensify, and "ultimately lead to the pathological state known as addiction". — [Koob & Volkow 2010, PMC2805560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2805560); [UNIAD summary](https://www.uniad.org.br/artigos/2-artigos-internacionais/neurocircuitry-of-addiction/)
- Circuits by stage, from Koob & Volkow's Lancet Psychiatry 2016 neurobiology review:
  - Binge/intoxication: rewarding effects, incentive salience and habit formation involve changes in dopamine and opioid peptides in the basal ganglia.
  - Withdrawal/negative affect: involves decreased function of the dopamine reward component and recruitment of brain stress systems in the extended amygdala.
  - Preoccupation/anticipation: involves dysregulation of prefrontal cortex and insula afferents, including glutamate, to the basal ganglia and extended amygdala.
  - [Koob & Volkow 2016, PMC6135092](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6135092/)
- Volkow has said the three stages "are not necessarily distinct" and can blend into one another. — [Fox News report on Surgeon General's report / NEJM review](https://www.foxnews.com/health/addiction-changes-brain-biology-in-3-stages-experts-say)
- In the withdrawal/negative-affect stage, both the HPA-axis stress response and the brain's (extrahypothalamic) stress response are activated. — [Koob & Volkow 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6135092/)
- **Dynorphin/KOR ("anti-reward")**:
  - Repeated ethanol intake raises dynorphin/KOR levels in the extended amygdala, which supports negative reinforcement. A critical review notes that findings on the direction of these effects are mixed. — [Drexel critical review](https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-critical-review-of-effects-on/991020418417004721)
  - In mice, chronic alcohol exposure and stress both engage extended-amygdala DYN/KOR to mediate stress-enhanced drinking. — [Haun et al. 2022](https://waggonercenter.utexas.edu/sites/default/files/documents/Haun%20et%20al%202022%20dynorphin%20KOR%20in%20ext%20amygdala%20stress%20enhanced%20alcohol%20drinking_1.pdf)
  - KOR antagonism in the extended amygdala reduces withdrawal-induced escalation of self-administration in dependent rats. — [Walker lab publications](https://healthscholars.usf.edu/walker-lab/publications)
  - Overexpressing Oprk1 in the VTA reproduces escalated self-administration and depressive-like behavior. — [PMC12893421](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12893421/)
- **CRF**: In alcohol-preferring msP rats, CRF/CRF1 is "tightly linked to the regulation of opioidergic mechanisms". In the BNST, alcohol intake increased CRF together with opioid receptor transcripts. — [PMC3396024 / msP rat study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396024)
- **GABA-A downregulation**:
  - Chronic alcohol decreases GABA neurotransmission through "down-regulation": fewer GABA-A receptors and changes in subunit composition. — [Michalak & Biała 2016, Acta Pol Pharm](https://www.ptfarm.pl/pub/File/Acta_Poloniae/2016/003.pdf)
  - Dependence involves time-dependent changes in GABA-A receptor density and subunit expression that produce a withdrawal-related deficit in GABA-A function. Cortical GABA levels are *not* reduced in acute withdrawal. — [PubMed 16952998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16952998/)
- **NMDA upregulation**: Ethanol potently inhibits NMDA receptors. Long-term exposure produces compensatory "up-regulation" of NMDAR function, which contributes to tolerance, dependence, and acute and late withdrawal signs. — [Nagy 2008, Current Neuropharmacology](https://www.benthamscience.com/article/11475)
- Alcohol dependence is associated with tolerance and withdrawal, with GABA-A and NMDA neuroadaptations playing key roles. In animal models, repeated withdrawal bouts worsen cognitive impairment. — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2019 (fpsyt-10-00339)](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/84684/)

### Inferences
- The model explains a shift from **positive reinforcement** (drinking for the dopamine/opioid "high") to **negative reinforcement** (drinking to relieve the dysphoria driven by CRF/dynorphin). This is why late-stage AUD looks more compulsive than hedonic. It also gives the rationale for drugs that target each stage:
  - naltrexone and nalmefene act on opioid reward (stage 1);
  - acamprosate and gabapentin target glutamate/GABA dysregulation (stage 2);
  - KOR and CRF1 antagonists are investigational for stage 2.
- **[background, unverified]** "Anti-reward" and "allostasis" are Koob's own terms (Koob & Le Moal). CRF1 antagonists such as pexacerfont and verucerfont failed in human AUD trials despite strong rodent data. This is a known translational gap.
- **[background, unverified]** Kindling: repeated withdrawal episodes progressively sensitize the brain, making later withdrawals more severe, with higher seizure risk. This is one reason repeated unmanaged "detoxes" are dangerous. It is consistent with ASAM's listing of "numerous prior withdrawal episodes" as a risk factor for complicated withdrawal (see Section 3).

### Gaps
- No primary source on kindling in humans (Ballenger & Post 1978; Becker reviews) was retrieved. Searches returned nothing kindling-specific.
- Exact Koob & Volkow wording on CRF and dynorphin as "between-system" anti-reward neuroadaptations was not fetched, because PMC was blocked.

## 2. Diagnosis and genetics: DSM-5, ICD-11, AUDIT, heritability, GWAS

### Takeaway
DSM-5 diagnoses AUD when at least 2 of 11 criteria are met within 12 months:
- mild: 2–3 criteria
- moderate: 4–5 criteria
- severe: 6 or more criteria

ICD-11 uses "alcohol dependence", based on 2 of 3 core feature groupings, and keeps a separate "harmful pattern" category. AUDIT is a 10-item screening tool, not a diagnostic one. Twin and adoption studies put heritability of alcohol dependence at about 50%. SNP-based heritability is only about 5–10%, and the largest multi-ancestry GWAS (over 1 million people) found 110 risk variants.

### Cited Findings
- DSM-5: AUD is "a problematic pattern of alcohol use leading to clinically significant impairment or distress" shown by at least 2 of 11 criteria within 12 months. Severity:
  - mild = 2–3 criteria
  - moderate = 4–5 criteria
  - severe = 6 or more criteria
  - [GPnotebook](https://gpnotebook.com/pages/psychiatry/diagnostic-and-statistical-manuals-of-mental-disorders-dsm-classification-of-alcohol-use-disorder-aud); [American Counseling Association brief](https://www.counseling.org/resources/practice-briefs/article/alcohol-user-disorder)
- Changes from DSM-IV: DSM-5 merged abuse and dependence, **added craving** and **dropped legal problems**. — [PsychCentral on DSM-5 changes](https://www.psychcentral.com/pro/dsm-5-changes-addiction-substance-related-disorders-alcoholism)
- ICD-11 "alcohol dependence":
  - The number of criteria does not determine the diagnosis. Features are grouped into 3 clusters (impaired control; increasing priority of alcohol; physiological features such as tolerance and withdrawal), and 2 of the 3 are required.
  - Features usually appear over at least 12 months, but the diagnosis can be made after 3 months of continuous (daily or near-daily) use.
  - [Redalyc / Rev Cient Cienc Salud 2024](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/7498/749878872014/749878872014.pdf)
- Agreement between ICD-11 and DSM-5 for AUD is "substantial but less than almost perfect". Dependence prevalence was highest under ICD-11. — [BJPsych Open concordance study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/study-on-concordance-of-icd11-icd10-and-dsm5-diagnostic-guidelines-for-alcohol-and-opioid-use-disorders/5969D49A2501FC6AEFEDA7B2F6328660)
- AUDIT has 10 items covering hazardous use, harmful use and dependence symptoms. It is "not sufficient or appropriate for use as a stand-alone diagnostic tool". — [American Counseling Association](https://www.counseling.org/resources/practice-briefs/article/alcohol-user-disorder)
- Heritability and GWAS:
  - Twin and adoption studies show about half of the risk of alcohol dependence is heritable. — [Kranzler et al. 2019, Nat Commun (MVP GWAS, N=274,424)](https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-019-09480-8)
  - The 2019 MVP GWAS found 18 loci: 5 shared between AUDIT-C and AUD diagnosis, 8 for AUDIT-C only and 5 for AUD only. — [Kranzler et al. 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6445072/)
  - SNP-based heritability of AUD and problematic alcohol use (PAU) is about 5.6–10.0%, i.e. "substantial missing heritability". A 2023 multi-ancestry GWAS of more than 1 million people found **110 risk variants**. — [Zhou et al. 2023, Nature Medicine (PMC10719093)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10719093)
  - A later MVP preprint reports 26 AUD loci and 22 AUDIT-C loci. MTAG combining maximum habitual intake with PAU gives 31 genome-wide significant loci. — [medRxiv 2022.05.02.22274580](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.05.02.22274580)

### Inferences
- The gap between ~50% twin heritability and ~5–10% SNP heritability reflects many small-effect common variants plus rare variants, gene–environment interaction, and limits of current arrays. Polygenic scores are not yet clinically useful for individual AUD prediction.
- **[background, unverified]** The strongest replicated loci are alcohol-metabolism genes:
  - ADH1B (rs1229984): the fast-metabolizing allele is protective.
  - ALDH2 (rs671): the East Asian "flush" variant is strongly protective.
  - Other loci with brain expression include DRD2, GCKR and FTO.
  - These findings are relevant to Chinese and East Asian readers.
- **[background, unverified]** The WHO AUDIT cutoff is commonly ≥8 for hazardous/harmful use. AUDIT-C (3 items) cutoffs are about ≥4 for men and ≥3 for women. These figures were not confirmed this session.

### Gaps
- No source was retrieved confirming the AUDIT ≥8 cutoff (WHO 2001 manual), or the full list of the 11 DSM-5 criteria from NIAAA, which was blocked.
- Gene identities for the 110 variants (Zhou 2023) were not retrieved.

## 3. Alcohol withdrawal: timeline, seizures, delirium tremens, CIWA-Ar, benzodiazepines and phenobarbital, thiamine

### Takeaway
Withdrawal usually starts 6–48 hours after the last drink. Delirium tremens (DTs) typically begins 48–96 hours after the last drink. The 2020 ASAM guideline recommends:
- benzodiazepines as first line;
- phenobarbital as an acceptable alternative, with growing support in severe or complicated cases;
- carbamazepine or gabapentin for mild-moderate cases;
- PAWSS to predict risk of complicated withdrawal;
- CIWA-Ar to monitor severity, not to diagnose.

### Cited Findings
- Symptoms can appear within 6–48 hours after stopping alcohol and may progress to DTs if untreated. DT onset is "usually 48–96 hr after last drink". The course is "variable, non-linear", and stages may overlap or be skipped. — [VUMC Alcohol Withdrawal Prevention Guideline v1.2026](https://www.vumc.org/trauma-and-scc/sites/default/files/public_files/Protocols/Alcohol-Withdrawal-Prevention-Guideline-v1.2026.pdf); [UAB benzodiazepine-sparing guideline 2025](https://uab.edu/medicine/surgery/images/Trauma_and_Acute_Care_Surgery/Protocols/Alcohol_Withdrawal_Guideline.BDZ_Sparing.Final.CLEAN_011625.pdf); [Advocate Health CE 2026](https://ce.advocatehealth.org/sites/default/files/media/2026-05/Francis%2C%20Patel%205.19%20CE.pdf)
- ASAM Clinical Practice Guideline on Alcohol Withdrawal Management (J Addict Med 2020;14(3S Suppl):1–72):
  - Definitions: "severe AWS" means severe signs and symptoms; "complicated AWS" means withdrawal with seizures or delirium.
  - CIWA-Ar was developed to monitor response to treatment, not for diagnosis.
  - PAWSS identifies patients at risk of complicated withdrawal.
  - Risk factors for complicated withdrawal: history of withdrawal delirium or seizures, many prior withdrawal episodes, age 65 or older, and long duration of heavy, regular drinking.
  - [ebm.one summary of ASAM](https://ebm.one/en/node/1597); [J Hosp Med review (Ganatra)](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/JHMVol16No12_Ganatra08011215e.PDF)
- Medication choice under ASAM:
  - Benzodiazepines are first line.
  - Phenobarbital is an acceptable alternative for severe withdrawal. Reviewers argued for a stronger recommendation in withdrawal delirium.
  - Carbamazepine or gabapentin are alternatives.
  - Alpha-2 agonists should **not** be used to treat withdrawal delirium, but can be ICU adjuncts for resistant withdrawal.
  - A secondary slide gives thresholds: CIWA-Ar 10–18, benzodiazepines first line; CIWA-Ar ≥19, benzodiazepines first line with phenobarbital, carbamazepine or gabapentin as alternatives. I could not verify these thresholds against the primary document.
  - [J Hosp Med review](https://cdn.mdedge.com/files/s3fs-public/JHMVol16No12_Ganatra08011215e.PDF); [Montana TQIP](https://mtqip.org/files/alcohol-withdrawal)
- Phenobarbital dosing varies by institution:
  - One protocol uses 130 mg PO every hour × 2 and caps the cumulative dose at 30 mg/kg. — [UAB BZD-sparing guideline](https://uab.edu/medicine/surgery/images/Trauma_and_Acute_Care_Surgery/Protocols/Alcohol_Withdrawal_Guideline.BDZ_Sparing.Final.CLEAN_011625.pdf)
  - An older protocol uses 120–240 mg IV for CIWA-Ar >15, up to 1–2 g total, and notes that phenytoin is ineffective for alcohol withdrawal seizures. — [Cree Health protocol 2013](https://www.creehealth.org/sites/default/files/Alcohol%20Withdrawl%20Management%20protocol%20Oct%2023%202013%20pharm%20exec%20approved.pdf)
- Recent (2025–2026) US trauma and surgery protocols (UAB, VUMC) are explicitly "benzodiazepine-sparing" and phenobarbital-based. This shows phenobarbital adoption in practice. — [UAB](https://uab.edu/medicine/surgery/images/Trauma_and_Acute_Care_Surgery/Protocols/Alcohol_Withdrawal_Guideline.BDZ_Sparing.Final.CLEAN_011625.pdf); [VUMC v1.2026](https://www.vumc.org/trauma-and-scc/sites/default/files/public_files/Protocols/Alcohol-Withdrawal-Prevention-Guideline-v1.2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[background, unverified]** Classic withdrawal timeline:
  - Minor withdrawal (tremor, anxiety, sweating, tachycardia): 6–24 h.
  - Alcoholic hallucinosis: 12–48 h, with a clear sensorium.
  - Withdrawal seizures: 6–48 h, peak about 24 h, usually generalized tonic-clonic.
  - DTs: 48–96 h, lasting up to 5–7 days, in about 3–5% of hospitalized withdrawal patients. Historical mortality was up to ~15–37% untreated and is now about 1–5% with treatment.
- **[background, unverified]** Thiamine should be given before glucose to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy. Typical doses are 100 mg/day for prophylaxis and much higher parenteral doses (e.g. 200–500 mg IV three times daily) when Wernicke's is suspected. The ASAM guideline recommends thiamine for all patients.
- **[background, unverified]** Benzodiazepine strategies are symptom-triggered dosing (CIWA-Ar ≥8–10), fixed-schedule dosing, or front-loading. Long-acting agents (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide) are preferred. Lorazepam or oxazepam are preferred in liver failure or the elderly. Symptom-triggered dosing reduces total benzodiazepine dose and treatment duration.
- Rising phenobarbital use is consistent with the neurobiology in Section 1: barbiturates act on GABA-A independently of benzodiazepines, and the NMDA blockade reported for them may help with benzodiazepine-resistant withdrawal.

### Gaps
- Primary ASAM 2020 text, NEJM review figures for DT incidence and mortality, and thiamine dosing were not retrieved directly, because those hosts were blocked.
- No 2025–2026 RCT comparing phenobarbital with benzodiazepines was identified.

## 4. Approved medications (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram, nalmefene), efficacy/NNT, underuse; off-label options (topiramate, gabapentin, baclofen, varenicline)

### Takeaway
The approved and best-supported medications have modest but real effects: NNT about 12 for acamprosate (return to any drinking) and about 12 for oral naltrexone (return to heavy drinking). They are rarely used. In 2023 only about **1.9%** of the 28.9 million Americans with AUD received medication. Among off-label drugs, topiramate has moderate evidence and more side effects. Gabapentin's evidence is low strength. Baclofen's results are mixed. Varenicline mainly reduces craving and does not clearly change drinking.

### Cited Findings
- **FDA-approved drugs**: disulfiram (1949, the first), naltrexone (oral 1994; extended-release injectable later) and acamprosate (2004). — [NIAAA Medications Development Program](https://www.niaaa.nih.gov/research/major-initiatives/medications-development-program); [Psychiatric News](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.pn.2018.pp1b4)
- **Jonas et al., JAMA 2014** (AHRQ meta-analysis, 122 RCTs + 1 cohort, N = 22,803):
  - NNT to prevent return to **any drinking**: acamprosate 12; oral naltrexone 50 mg/day 20.
  - NNT to prevent return to **heavy drinking**: oral naltrexone 12.
  - Acamprosate and naltrexone did not differ significantly (RD 0.02; 95% CI −0.03 to 0.08).
  - Injectable naltrexone was not associated with return to any drinking, but reduced heavy drinking days (WMD −4.6%; 95% CI −8.5% to −0.56%).
  - Number needed to harm (withdrawal due to adverse events): 48 for naltrexone and 12 for nalmefene. Not significantly increased for acamprosate or topiramate.
  - Topiramate and nalmefene also reduced consumption.
  - Fewer than one-third of people with AUD receive treatment, and fewer than 10% receive medication.
  - [JAMA 2014;311(18):1889](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/1869208); [PubMed 24825644](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24825644/); [BU CARE PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/care/files/2014/04/Jonas-et-al-2014.pdf)
- **2022 network meta-analysis** (McPheeters et al., JAMA Netw Open; summarized by BU):
  - Acamprosate, disulfiram, baclofen and oral naltrexone had the best evidence for improving abstinence.
  - Baclofen reduced heavy drinking (RR 0.57). It was one of only two drugs that improved both drinking outcomes without excess dropouts for adverse effects.
  - [BU AOD Health summary](https://www.bu.edu/aodhealth/2022/10/25/effectiveness-and-adverse-events-of-medications-for-alcohol-use-disorder); [UIC DIG FAQ](https://dig.pharmacy.uic.edu/faqs/2024-2/january-2024-faqs/what-information-is-available-on-the-off-label-use-of-gabapentin-and-topiramate-for-alcohol-use-disorder/)
- **APA practice guideline (2018)**:
  - Recommends naltrexone or acamprosate for moderate to severe AUD.
  - Suggests topiramate or gabapentin when patients prefer them or cannot tolerate, or have not responded to, first-line drugs.
  - Disulfiram is not first line.
  - Naltrexone is contraindicated with current or anticipated opioid use. Acamprosate is contraindicated in severe renal impairment.
  - [Psychiatric News](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/full/10.1176/appi.pn.2018.pp1b4); [UIC DIG](https://dig.pharmacy.uic.edu/faqs/2024-2/january-2024-faqs/what-information-is-available-on-the-off-label-use-of-gabapentin-and-topiramate-for-alcohol-use-disorder/)
- Topiramate has moderate strength of evidence but more adverse effects. Gabapentin has low strength of evidence. — [UIC DIG](https://dig.pharmacy.uic.edu/faqs/2024-2/january-2024-faqs/what-information-is-available-on-the-off-label-use-of-gabapentin-and-topiramate-for-alcohol-use-disorder/)
- **Nalmefene** (approved by the EMA in 2013 for "as-needed" drinking reduction, not FDA-approved for AUD):
  - A network meta-analysis found it beat placebo on heavy drinking days and total alcohol consumption long-term, but not short-term, with more adverse events. — [J Clin Psychiatry / network MA](https://dev2.psychiatrist.com/pdf/173849)
  - A 2016 aggregated-data analysis found no benefit on mortality or quality of life. Several bodies concluded it has no proven advantage over naltrexone. — [PubMed 27842940](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27842940/); [MedicalResearch.com](https://medicalresearch.com/?p=20347)
- **Baclofen**: evidence conflicts.
  - A 2022 meta-analysis found fewer heavy drinking days, but no effect on time to relapse, craving or drink counts. — [IJPS updated meta-analysis](https://www.ijpsonline.com/abstract/efficacy-and-safety-of-baclofen-for-treatment-of-alcohol-dependence-an-updated-metaanalysis-and-metaregression-study-4507.html)
  - An earlier meta-analysis of 14 RCTs found no significant benefit on primary outcomes.
- **Varenicline** (2020 meta-analysis): no significant differences in percent heavy drinking days, drinks per drinking day or percent days abstinent. Craving decreased significantly. — [J Clin Psychiatry 2020](https://dev2.psychiatrist.com/jcp/varenicline-for-alcohol-use-disorders)
- **Underuse** (NIAAA, from NSDUH 2023):
  - Of about 28.9 million people aged 12 or older with past-year AUD, only **1.9% (554,000)** received medication for AUD.
  - Adults: 2.0% (552,000 of 28.1 million).
  - Ages 12–17: 0.3%.
  - [NIAAA: Alcohol Treatment in the United States](https://niaaa.nih.gov/alcohols-effects-health/alcohol-topics/alcohol-facts-and-statistics/alcohol-treatment-united-states)
  - NSDUH 2022: 2.1% of 29.5 million. — [National Council](https://www.thenationalcouncil.org/alcohol-consumption-sobering-problem/)
  - A NIH Research Matters piece also reports few people prescribed medication. — [NIH Research Matters](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/few-are-prescribed-medications-treat-alcohol-problems)
  - Few US mental health facilities offer AUD medications. — [Psychiatric News Alert 2025](https://alert.psychnews.org/2025/07/few-mental-health-facilities-offer.html)
  - Medications were started in only about 30% of VA AUD hospitalizations. — [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/mental-health/medications-initiated-in-30-percent-of-hospitalizations-for-alcohol-use-disorder-among-veterans)

### Inferences
- Efficacy is modest (NNT about 9–20) but comparable to many accepted psychiatric and medical drugs (e.g. antidepressants). Use below 2% is a care-delivery failure, not an evidence gap.
- **[background, unverified]** Mechanisms:
  - naltrexone: mu-opioid antagonist that blunts alcohol reward (stage 1);
  - nalmefene: mu/delta antagonist and partial kappa agonist;
  - acamprosate: thought to modulate NMDA/glutamate and restore balance in protracted withdrawal (stage 2/3), cleared renally;
  - disulfiram: aldehyde dehydrogenase inhibitor that causes acetaldehyde aversion, and works only with supervised adherence.
- Naltrexone is often preferred when the goal is to reduce heavy drinking. Acamprosate fits patients already abstinent, and those with liver disease, since it is not hepatically metabolized.

### Gaps
- The Cochrane 2010 acamprosate and naltrexone reviews (Rösner et al.) and Cochrane baclofen 2023 (Minozzi) were not retrieved.
- European and Chinese prescribing rates were not found.
- No 2024–2026 update of the Jonas NNTs was retrieved.

## 5. Emerging therapies: GLP-1 receptor agonists, psilocybin, other pipeline (as of 2026)

### Takeaway
GLP-1 receptor agonists are the most important emerging candidates:
- One small phase 2 RCT of semaglutide (Hendershot et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2025, n=48) reduced lab-measured drinking, drinks per drinking day and craving.
- Large observational studies (Sweden 2024; US 2026 BMJ Open, including tirzepatide) link semaglutide and liraglutide to about 28–36% lower AUD hospitalization risk.
- No GLP-1 drug is approved for AUD, and no phase 3 result exists as of Oct 2026.

Psilocybin results are mixed: positive in the 2022 Bogenschutz trial, null for single-dose relapse prevention in 2025. Other phase 2 candidates include apremilast, sunobinop and ibudilast.

### Cited Findings
- **Hendershot et al., "Once-Weekly Semaglutide in Adults With AUD: A Randomized Clinical Trial", JAMA Psychiatry, Feb 2025 (doi:10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2024.4789)**:
  - Design: phase 2, double-blind, placebo-controlled; 48 non-treatment-seeking adults; 9 weeks of low-dose titration (0.25 to 1.0 mg/week); no psychosocial treatment.
  - Results:
    - Reduced alcohol consumed in a post-treatment laboratory self-administration session.
    - Reduced drinks per drinking day (p = 0.04) and weekly craving (p = 0.01).
    - Larger reductions in heavy drinking days than placebo.
    - No effect on number of drinking days or drinks per calendar day.
    - Drinks per drinking day fell by nearly 30% versus about 2% on placebo by month 2 (press figure).
    - Exploratory: fewer cigarettes per day among smokers.
  - Authors urge larger, longer trials.
  - [PMC11822619](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11822619/); [USC news](https://today.usc.edu/popular-weight-loss-diabetes-drug-shows-promise-in-reducing-cravings-for-alcohol/); [Science News](https://sciencenews.org/?p=3150235); [Bariatric News](https://bariatricnews.net/post/clinical-trial-confirms-semaglutide-reduces-cravings-for-alcohol-and-heavy-drinking)
- **Lähteenvuo et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2025;82(1)** (online Nov 2024):
  - Design: Swedish nationwide registers, 2006–2023; 227,866 people aged 16–64 with AUD; within-individual Cox analysis comparing each person's periods on and off drug.
  - AUD hospitalization: semaglutide aHR 0.64; liraglutide aHR 0.72.
  - Substance use disorder hospitalization: semaglutide aHR 0.68; liraglutide aHR 0.78.
  - Other GLP-1 agonists showed no association.
  - [EMJ](https://www.emjreviews.com/diabetes/news/glp-1-agonists-may-help-reduce-alcohol-related-hospitalisations); [BU AOD Health](https://www.bu.edu/aodhealth/?p=5205); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/semaglutide-and-liraglutide-linked-to-lower-alcohol-related-hospitalization-risk/)

### Inferences
- GLP-1 effects in the Hendershot RCT show up in *amount* per drinking occasion and craving, not *frequency*. This fits a mechanism of reduced reward value, i.e. blunted mesolimbic dopamine response (stage 1), more than reduced negative-affect drinking.
- The Swedish within-person design reduces between-person confounding. Residual confounding (people starting GLP-1s may be healthier or more engaged with care), the hospitalization-only outcome, and an obesity/T2D-heavy population limit generalization.
- The field needs adequately powered, treatment-seeking phase 3 RCTs with drinking-outcome endpoints and head-to-head comparisons with naltrexone. None have reported as of Oct 2026, based on what was found.
- **[background, unverified]** Earlier GLP-1 RCTs had mixed results:
  - Exenatide (Klausen et al., JCI Insight 2022, n = 127): no effect on heavy drinking days overall, but reduced drinking in a subgroup with obesity (BMI >30).
  - A dulaglutide trial (2025) reportedly reduced alcohol intake in smokers.
  - Novo Nordisk–sponsored and academic semaglutide AUD and liver disease trials are ongoing.

## 6. Psychosocial treatment: CBT, motivational interviewing, contingency management, Alcoholics Anonymous (Cochrane 2020), harm reduction

### Takeaway
The 2020 Cochrane review found **high-certainty evidence** that manualized Twelve-Step Facilitation (TSF) and AA produce higher continuous abstinence than other established treatments such as CBT (NNT about 13.6 at 12 months), at lower cost. Effects on other drinking outcomes were comparable. Contingency management shows the largest effect sizes, but alcohol-specific trials are few, partly because alcohol is hard to verify biologically. CBT and MI effects are small (g about 0.15–0.2) against control conditions.

### Cited Findings
- **Kelly, Humphreys & Ferri, Cochrane 2020 (CD012880.pub2)**:
  - Scope: 27 studies, 10,565 participants.
  - Abstinence: high-certainty evidence that manualized AA/TSF raises continuous abstinence more than other active treatments, including CBT. About 42% versus 35% were continuously abstinent at 1 year.
  - Other outcomes: TSF performs at least as well on drinking intensity, consequences and addiction severity.
  - Costs: 4 of 5 economic studies showed substantial healthcare cost savings.
  - Benefit comes mainly from increased AA participation that continues after formal TSF ends.
  - [Cochrane news](https://www.cochrane.org/news/new-cochrane-review-finds-alcoholics-anonymous-and-12-step-facilitation-programs-help-people); [Wiley press release](https://investors.wiley.com/news/news-details/2020/New-Cochrane-Review-finds-Alcoholics-Anonymous-and-Twelve-Step-Facilitation-programs-help-people-to-recover-from-alcohol-problems-2020-3-11/default.aspx); [Distillation, PMC8060988](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8060988)
- **AFP "Cochrane for Clinicians" (2021)**: manualized 12-step programs improved continuous abstinence at 12 months (NNT 13.6), with effects persisting at 24 and 36 months. — [AAFP 2021;103(5):272](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2021/0301/p272.html)
- **CBT**:
  - A 2009 meta-analysis of 53 studies found a small overall effect (g = 0.15). Superior effects specific to AUD were not replicated. Differences against other manualized therapies were small. — [Magill & Ray 2009 (PMC2897895)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2897895/)
  - CBT/MI for comorbid AUD and depression: g = 0.17 (95% CI 0.07–0.28) on drinking versus usual care. — [Riper et al. 2014 (PMC4227588)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4227588)
- **MI**: brief MI effects are about 0.2, small to moderate versus no treatment for alcohol. — [Cambridge BJPsych Advances: "Drug and alcohol addiction: do psychosocial treatments work?"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/0B02DC27CE3CEC0096DF5397A4282CBB/S2056467800000645a.pdf/drug-and-alcohol-addiction-do-psychosocial-treatments-work.pdf)
- **Contingency management**:
  - A 2017 follow-up meta-analysis (84 studies, all substances) found CM significant at 3-month follow-up only, and MI significant at 6 months only. — [Sayegh et al. 2017, PubMed 28437121](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28437121/)
  - An alcohol-specific meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (n = 459) found a pooled effect of 0.992 (95% CI 0.654–1.331). This is a Norwegian thesis, not peer-reviewed, so treat with caution. — [OsloMet](https://oda.oslomet.no/oda-xmlui/handle/11250/3133587?show=full)

### Inferences
- AA's advantage is specific to **abstinence**. For patients with drinking-reduction goals, CBT, MI and medications are equally reasonable. Combined medication plus behavioral support is standard.
- **[background, unverified]**
  - The COMBINE trial (JAMA 2006) found naltrexone plus medical management as effective as combined behavioral intervention.
  - Project MATCH found CBT, MET and TSF broadly equivalent.
  - CM for alcohol is now feasible with remote breathalyzers or PEth/EtG biomarkers.
- **[background, unverified]** Harm-reduction approaches:
  - controlled or reduced-drinking goals, now accepted as outcomes. FDA guidance recognizes no heavy drinking days, and WHO risk-level reductions are used as trial endpoints;
  - nalmefene as-needed dosing;
  - managed alcohol programs for unhoused people with severe AUD (Canada);
  - Sinclair-method naltrexone.
  - No sourced findings on these were retrieved this session.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved for harm-reduction outcomes: managed alcohol programs, the FDA 2015 draft guidance accepting the "no heavy drinking days" endpoint, or WHO risk-drinking-level reductions.
- No peer-reviewed, alcohol-specific CM meta-analysis was confirmed. Some exist (e.g. in Addiction), but they were not retrieved.
- The APA 2018 guideline's psychosocial recommendations were not retrieved.
